blog 1: foundations of image registration#

This is my initial entry for a series of blogs intended to deepen my understanding of image registration applied to astrophotography. Based heavily off of Gregory Gundersen's blog.

introduction: what is image registration?#

Image registration is the process of finding an appropriate transformation T given two images: a fixed image \(I_F\) and a moving image \((I_M\)), such that \(I_M\) is spatially aligned with \(I_F\).

I like to think of this process using an example from astronomical imaging. Given two mis-aligned images of the night sky, how do we find an appropriate series of transformations such that the two images stacked present a natural image?

types of transformation models#

Depending on the registration problem at hand, we may choose different types of transformation models. A transformation model describes the mathematical framework that constrains how the moving image can be aligned with the fixed image. Put simply, the transformation model chosen determines the operations (e.g. rotation or shear) that we can apply to images for registration. One of the simplest transformation models is the rigid model, where we are limited to only translations and rotations, which preserve distances and angles. Another common model is the affine model which allows for translation, rotation, shearing and scaling. Affine transformations are more flexible than strict rigid transforms which may suit more complex registration tasks better.

We can represent our transformations through matrices. For example, 2D rotation can be formed this matrix where theta is our rotation degree in radians: $$ \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta) & -\sin(\theta) \\\ \sin(\theta) & \cos(\theta) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\\ y \end{pmatrix} $$

Unfortunately, if we want to perform translation, we need to deal with vector addition, which on modern hardware isn’t ideal. Specifically, let’s take the example where we have a rotation matrix \(R\), a scaling matrix \(S\), a translation vector \(t\) and we want to string together these operations. Given we are in 2D space, we would have to apply \(S(Rx)+t\), which involves two matrix multiplications and an addition where we have to keep track of the order of operations. If we could instead use only matrix multiplies, our hardware (GPUs are incredibly effective at matrix multiplies) could much more effectively apply our sequential transformations in an order like \((SRt)x\).

Conveniently, we can represent translation and rotation as a single matrix-vector operation:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & t_x \\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & t_y \\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\\ y \\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix} x' \\\ y' \\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

You might be asking why there is an extra one in our coordinate vector. We now have homogeneous coordinates where the one is our \(w\) coordinate which is different than our normal \(z\). The reason we do this is to give literal space in our matrices to embed translation with other linear transformations in a singular matrix.

Let’s go through a simple example reinforcing this idea. Given the coordinates \((1, 2), (3, 4)\) and \((5, 6)\), we can construct the matrix where each column represents a set of homogeneous coordinates:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

Let’s say that we want to rotate those coordinate columns by 90 degrees and translate two units right and two units north. We will set up our matrix-multiplication like this (keep in mind that 90 degrees is \(\pi/2\) in radians):

$$ \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\frac{\pi}{2}) & -\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}) & 2 \\\ \sin(\frac{\pi}{2}) & \cos(\frac{\pi}{2}) & 2 \\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & -4 \\\ 3 & 5 & 7 \\\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

Our new coordinates are \((0, 3), (-2, 5)\) and \((-4, 7)\).

posing image registration as an optimization problem#

Traditional image registration algorithms can be posed as an optimization problem, where given a cost function, we try and optimize the parameters and in turn minimize cost. Formally, let \( I_F \) and \(I_M\) represent our fixed and moving images respectively. In two-dimensional space, our image coordinates are represented by \(\mathbf{x}=(x,y)\) and \(\mathbf{x'}=(x',y')\). We want to approximate a transformation T : ℝd → ℝd (d is two in our case) such that IM(T(x)) ≈ IF(x), for all x ∈ Ω where Ω is our spatial domain (space in which our coordinates live). While thinking about how image coordinates are represented, I think it is interesting to touch on how the spatial domain of coordinates behaves more like an affine space than a strict vector space. Since translations, which we often perform during registration, break the origin preserving axiom of vector spaces, an affine space, which disregards the origin, is a more appropriate representation.

We formulate our optimization problem with the following equation: $$ T = \arg\min_T \mathcal{L}(I_F, I_M \circ T) $$

common loss functions#

There are a few loss functions that are utilized in traditional registration algorithms to optimize parameters. One of the most common cost functions for image registration is the sum of squared errors (SSE): $$ SSE = \mathcal{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}(y_i-f(x))^2 $$

where $y_i$ is the ground-truth and $f(x)$ is the predicted value. While this is the general formulation for the SSE, we will need to tune it slightly to suit our image registration task.

$$ \mathcal{L}_{SSE}=\mathcal{\sum_{(x,y)\in\Omega}}[I_F(x, y)-I_M(T_\theta(x, y))]^2 $$

Let \(I_F(x,y)\) be the fixed image and \(I_M(T_\theta(x, y))\) be the moving image with our transformation applied to its spatial domain. Comparing this to the traditional SSE formula, our fixed image is the “ground-truth” and we are trying to transform our moving image to decrease the loss metric. More specifically, we calculate the residual or error of the set of coordinates for both images at a specific coordinate.
