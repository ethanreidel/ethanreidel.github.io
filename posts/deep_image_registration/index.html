This is my initial entry for a series of blogs intended to deepen my understanding of image registration applied to astrophotography. Based heavily off of Gregory Gundersen's blog.

introduction: what is image registration?#

Image registration is the process of finding an appropriate transformation T given two images: a fixed image (IF) and a moving image (IM), such that IM is spatially aligned with IF. In general, we choose a fixed image IF and transform IM onto IF.

I like to think of this process using an example from astro imaging. Given two mis-aligned images of the night sky, how do we find an appropriate series of transformations such that the two images stacked present a natural image. I naturally think that this problem could be solved through deep learning algorithms. I will touch on that topic later. Back to the basics.

types of transformation models#

Depending on the problem at hand, we may choose different types of transformation models. A transformation model describes the mathematical framework that constrains how the moving image can be aligned with the fixed image. One of the simplest transformation models is the rigid transformation, where we are limited to only translations and rotations, which preserve distances and angles.

posing image registration as an optimization problem#

Traditional image registration algorithms can be posed as an optimization problem, where given a cost function, we try and optimize the output of it. Formally, let IF and IM represent our fixed and moving images respectively. In two-dimensional space, our image coordinates are represented by x = (x, y) and x' = (x', y'). We want to approximate a transformation T : ℝd → ℝd (d is two in our case) such that IM(T(x)) ≈ IF(x), for all x ∈ Ω where Ω is our spatial domain (space in which our coordinates live). While thinking about how image coordinates are represented, I think it is interesting to touch on how the spatial domain of coordinates behaves more like an affine space than a strict vector space. Since translations break the origin preserving axiom of vector spaces, an affine space, which disregards the origin, is a more appropriate representation.
